14. Expand the Boundary of Caring

Meadows (2001): Dancing with Systems

1 Expand the Boundary of Caring

Principle 14 from Meadows (2001)

Our caring has to encompass all parts of the system, including parts that are distant in time and space. Everything I do now affects systems far away and far in the future. Expand the boundary of your concern to include all who live in the system.

1.1 The Principle

Every system analysis begins with a choice about boundaries: What is inside the system? What is outside? Where does the system end and its environment begin?

This choice is not neutral. It determines:

  • Whose interests are represented in the analysis
  • Which feedback loops are visible and which are invisible
  • What costs are accounted for and what costs are externalized
  • What time horizon is considered relevant

Meadows argues that we consistently draw boundaries that are too narrow — that exclude people and places affected by the system who are distant in space or time. This systematic exclusion is not just an analytical error; it is a moral failure that compounds into injustice and unsustainability.

“Expanding the boundary of caring” means deliberately extending our circle of concern to include:

  • People in distant places who are affected by our actions
  • Future generations who will live with the consequences of current decisions
  • Non-human species and ecological systems that are affected by human activity
  • People currently excluded from power and representation in decision-making processes

1.2 Why Boundaries Matter

The boundaries we draw around systems shape what is visible. When costs are externalized beyond the boundary — to downstream communities, to future generations, to poorer countries — they do not appear in the analysis and do not factor into decisions. The system appears to perform well while actually shifting its costs onto those outside the accounting.

This is not hypothetical. Most major environmental and social problems involve the systematic externalization of costs beyond the boundary of caring:

  • Climate change involves emitting greenhouse gases in the present while the costs fall on future generations and on poorer countries that have contributed little to the problem

  • Pollution in one community often flows to others — downstream, downwind — who bear the health costs without having participated in the decision to pollute

  • Global supply chains can rely on labor practices, environmental conditions, and safety standards that would not be acceptable in the purchasing country — externalizing these conditions beyond the boundary of visibility

  • Deforestation may increase short-run profits for landowners while degrading water supplies, biodiversity, and climate regulation for distant and future populations

1.3 Boundaries as Choices

It is important to recognize that system boundaries are choices, not fixed features of reality. The choice to include or exclude a variable from a model, to count or not count a cost, to represent or not represent a stakeholder — these are choices with moral implications.

Economic analyses that ignore non-market ecosystem services are not objective — they encode a choice about what matters. Health impact assessments that consider only direct health effects of a policy, not indirect effects on social determinants, are not comprehensive — they encode a choice about scope.

Making boundary choices explicit, and asking who benefits and who is harmed by any particular choice of boundaries, is itself a form of expanding the boundary of caring.

1.4 Examples

1.4.1 Environmental justice

Environmental justice research has documented that industrial pollution, waste disposal sites, and other environmental burdens are systematically located in communities with less political power — communities of color and low-income communities. This pattern reflects a boundary choice: decision-makers about facility siting drew their boundaries of caring to exclude affected communities. Environmental justice movements demand that these communities be included in the boundary of analysis and decision-making.

1.4.2 Intergenerational equity

Many major sustainability challenges — climate change, debt, depletion of natural capital, decommissioning of nuclear facilities — involve transferring costs to future generations. Future people cannot vote, cannot lobby, and cannot participate in current decisions. Expanding the boundary of caring requires inventing institutional mechanisms to represent their interests — constitutional environmental provisions, long-term budget rules, sustainability accounting frameworks.

1.4.3 Animal welfare

Modern industrial agriculture produces enormous quantities of food at low cost by excluding the welfare of animals from the boundary of analysis. The suffering of confined animals is real, but it is invisible in the accounting because animals are outside the boundary of economic concern. Expanding the boundary of caring means asking whether that exclusion is justified.

1.4.4 One Health

The One Health framework in medicine explicitly expands the boundary of health analysis to include human health, animal health, and ecosystem health as interconnected. This boundary expansion reflects the recognition that human infectious diseases, antibiotic resistance, food safety, and climate health risks cannot be understood or managed while restricting attention to human health alone.

NoteThe Systems Basis of Ethics

Meadows’ principle connects systems analysis to ethics. The choice of system boundary is not merely an analytical decision — it is an ethical one. Drawing boundaries that systematically exclude the interests of some people, future generations, or non-human species is a form of injustice that systems analysis makes visible.

Systems thinking, at its deepest level, asks us to take seriously the effects of our actions on all parts of the systems we inhabit — including the parts that are far away, weak, and without voice.

1.5 Practical Application

  1. Ask: Who is outside the current boundary of analysis? Who bears the costs or consequences of this system’s behavior but is not represented in the analysis or decision-making?

  2. Map the externalities. What costs, risks, or consequences of this system are being borne by people or systems outside the current boundary? What would have to change if they were internalized?

  3. Include future time periods. What are the consequences of current decisions in 20, 50, or 100 years? Who will bear them? Can they be represented in current decisions?

  4. Expand stakeholder representation. Who are the affected communities, organizations, and interest groups? Are those who bear costs represented in decision-making processes?

TipKey Takeaway

The boundary of analysis is always a choice, and narrow boundaries systematically externalize costs onto those without power or voice. Expanding the boundary of caring means deliberately including in analysis and decision-making all those affected by the system — near and far, present and future, human and non-human.

1.6 Discussion Questions

  1. Think of a system you analyze or manage. Who is currently outside the boundary of analysis? What costs or consequences are being externalized onto them?

  2. Meadows connects systems analysis to ethics through the concept of boundary choice. Is this connection legitimate — is boundary choice an ethical question — or is it a confusion of analytical and moral categories?

  3. What institutional mechanisms could give voice to future generations and distant communities in current decision-making? What would make these mechanisms effective?
