2. Expose Your Mental Models

Meadows (2001): Dancing with Systems

1 Expose Your Mental Models to the Light of Day

Principle 2 from Meadows (2001)

Remember, always, that everything you know, and everything everyone knows, is only a model. Get your model out there where it can be viewed. Invite others to challenge your assumptions and add their own.

1.1 The Principle

Every decision we make is shaped by an implicit model of how the world works. These mental models are not neutral descriptions of reality — they are selective, simplified representations that highlight some features of a system while hiding others.

Mental models include:

  • Assumptions about causal relationships (“if we lower the price, demand will increase”)
  • Beliefs about boundaries (“that’s outside our control”)
  • Values embedded in what we measure and optimize for
  • Time horizons (how far ahead we think)
  • Assumptions about what stays constant while other things change

Meadows’ counsel is to make these implicit models explicit — to write them down, draw them as diagrams, share them with others, and subject them to examination.

1.2 Why It Matters

Hidden mental models are dangerous for several reasons:

  • They can be wrong. All models are simplifications; some simplifications omit crucial dynamics. An implicit model can’t be corrected because no one knows it exists.

  • They vary across people. When a team makes decisions, each member is likely working from a different mental model. Without making these explicit, disagreements about actions are really disagreements about models — and can’t be resolved until the underlying models are visible.

  • They embed values. Choosing to model a forest as “timber board-feet per acre” rather than as “a living ecosystem” encodes a value judgment in the model itself.

  • They determine what gets optimized. You can only optimize what you measure, and what you measure reflects your model.

1.3 Tools for Exposing Mental Models

1.3.1 Causal Loop Diagrams

A causal loop diagram (CLD) shows variables connected by arrows labeled with the direction of causal influence (positive or negative) and the resulting feedback loops. Drawing a CLD forces you to articulate assumed causal relationships and makes them visible to others.

Example: A simple CLD for a population system might show:

  • Population → (+) → Births → (+) → Population (reinforcing loop)
  • Population → (+) → Deaths → (-) → Population (balancing loop)

1.3.2 Stock-and-Flow Diagrams

Stock-and-flow diagrams make quantitative assumptions explicit — what are the stocks? what flows in and out? what controls the rates?

1.3.3 Scenario Planning

Scenario planning surfaces assumptions about the future by explicitly articulating alternative trajectories. When different people construct different scenarios, the differences reveal different mental models.

1.3.4 Pre-mortems

A pre-mortem asks: “Imagine this project has failed. What happened?” This surfaces implicit assumptions about what could go wrong — assumptions that would otherwise remain unexamined.

1.4 Examples

1.4.1 Urban traffic planning

Traffic engineers modeled congestion as a problem of insufficient road capacity. Their mental model: more roads → less congestion. For decades, cities built more roads. But the model was wrong — more roads induced more driving (induced demand), and congestion remained. Only when planners exposed and questioned the underlying model did alternative approaches (congestion pricing, transit investment) become thinkable.

1.4.2 Corporate strategy

In the 1990s, many technology companies held an implicit mental model that internet traffic would grow without limit, justifying enormous infrastructure investment. When the model was finally made explicit — and challenged — it became clear that the growth assumptions were unsupportable, presaging the dot-com bust.

WarningThe Danger of “Everyone Knows”

The most dangerous mental models are those that go unquestioned because “everyone knows” they are true. Paradigm-level assumptions are rarely articulated and therefore rarely tested. Getting them out into the light is uncomfortable — but essential.

1.5 Practical Application

  1. Draw your model. Before a meeting about a problem, spend 15 minutes sketching a causal diagram of what you think is causing it. Bring it to share.

  2. Articulate your assumptions. For any policy recommendation, write down the 5–10 key assumptions the policy depends on. Ask: Which of these are most uncertain? Which would change the recommendation if wrong?

  3. Invite disagreement. When presenting a model, explicitly ask others where they think you are wrong. Treat challenges as gifts, not attacks.

  4. Compare models across stakeholders. When different groups consistently disagree on what to do, map out their underlying models. Often the disagreement is more about assumptions than about values.

TipKey Takeaway

Your mental model of a system is not reality — it is your theory of reality. Making that theory explicit is the first step toward improving it. Shared, visible models are better than private, hidden ones.

1.6 Discussion Questions

  1. Think of a mental model you hold about a system in your field. What key variables does it include? What does it leave out? What would change if you added those variables?

  2. Meadows says that mental models “are not maps of reality but maps of what we believe reality to be.” What is the difference, and why does it matter?

  3. What organizational or cultural forces make it difficult to expose mental models to scrutiny? How might those forces be overcome?

References
