3. Honor, Respect, and Distribute Information

Meadows (2001): Dancing with Systems

1 Honor, Respect, and Distribute Information

Principle 3 from Meadows (2001)

There is no substitute for accurate, timely, and relevant information. Information is power. Those who keep information to themselves gain power over others in the short term. Spread information for all it’s worth.

1.1 The Principle

Information is the nervous system of a complex system. Feedback loops — the mechanisms by which systems self-regulate and adapt — work only when accurate information flows freely. When information is blocked, delayed, distorted, or monopolized, feedback fails, and the system degrades.

Meadows identifies three dimensions of healthy information flows:

  • Accuracy: Is the information true? Are the measures meaningful?
  • Timeliness: Does information reach decision-makers while it can still affect their decisions?
  • Distribution: Does information reach those who need it and can act on it?

1.2 Why It Matters

1.2.1 Delayed information causes oscillation

One of the most common sources of system instability is a delay in the feedback loop. A decision-maker acts, but the effect of that action isn’t visible until later — by which time another decision has already been made. This produces the classic overshoot-and-oscillation pattern seen in:

  • Commodity price cycles (farmers plant more → prices fall → farmers plant less → prices rise → repeat)
  • Inventory management (bullwhip effect in supply chains)
  • Building booms and busts (long construction delays cause overbuilding)

1.2.2 Missing information causes mismanagement

You cannot manage what you cannot see. When important variables are not measured, or their measurement is hidden from decision-makers, those variables tend to be ignored in decision-making. The result is systematic neglect of crucial system properties.

Classic examples:

  • Businesses that externalize pollution costs don’t measure them and don’t include them in pricing — so they over-pollute relative to what would be efficient
  • Healthcare systems that don’t track outcome quality (only procedure volume) optimize for volume at the expense of outcomes

1.2.3 Monopolized information creates power asymmetries

When a subset of actors control information flows, they gain power over others. This power is often used in self-serving ways that damage the broader system.

1.3 Examples

1.3.1 Environmental disclosure

The U.S. Toxics Release Inventory (TRI), established in 1986, requires large manufacturers to publicly report their releases of toxic chemicals. Before TRI, this information was held internally and largely invisible to communities and regulators. After TRI, companies began voluntarily reducing emissions — not because the law required it, but because public disclosure created accountability. Information flow alone changed behavior.

1.3.2 Informed consent in medicine

The principle of informed consent ensures that patients receive accurate, complete information about their diagnosis and treatment options. When physicians withheld information (as was common historically, ostensibly to avoid causing distress), patients were disempowered and could not participate meaningfully in their own care. Information distribution enabled patient agency.

1.3.3 Nutrition labeling

Mandatory nutrition labels on food products created information flows that had not existed before. Consumers could compare products in ways previously impossible, food companies reformulated products to avoid unflattering comparisons, and public health outcomes gradually shifted. A simple structural change in information flow changed the dynamics of the food system.

WarningDistorted Information Is Worse than No Information

A feedback loop that carries inaccurate information can be more damaging than no feedback at all. Systems optimize toward their measured goals; if the measure is wrong, the system will optimize toward the wrong thing. This is sometimes called Goodhart’s Law: “When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.”

1.4 Practical Application

  1. Map the information flows in the system. Who knows what? Who needs to know what? Where are the gaps, delays, or distortions?

  2. Ask: What happens if this information is hidden? For each key piece of information, consider what decision-makers would do differently if they had it or lacked it.

  3. Design for transparency by default. Assume information should flow unless there is a specific reason to restrict it (privacy, security). Resist the cultural tendency to hoard information.

  4. Shorten feedback delays. Find ways to measure consequences sooner. Real-time dashboards, rapid learning cycles, and user feedback systems all shorten the time between action and feedback.

TipKey Takeaway

Systems degrade when information is late, missing, or distorted. Distributing accurate, timely information widely is one of the most powerful — and underutilized — interventions available in complex systems.

1.5 Discussion Questions

  1. Think of a system you are familiar with. What important information is not being measured, shared, or acted upon? What would change if it were?

  2. Meadows argues that information is power. Who currently controls key information flows in that system? Who benefits from the current arrangement?

  3. What are the limits of “more information”? Can information overload itself become a problem? How would you design an information architecture that provides what decision-makers need without overwhelming them?

References

Meadows, Donella H. 2001. “Dancing with Systems.” Whole Earth. https://demorrison.ucdavis.edu/files/Meadows_Systems_Thinking_2001.pdf.
