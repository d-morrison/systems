5. Pay Attention to What Is Important, Not Just What Is Quantifiable

Meadows (2001): Dancing with Systems

1 Pay Attention to What Is Important, Not Just What Is Quantifiable

Principle 5 from Meadows (2001)

Our culture, obsessed with numbers, has given us the idea that what we can measure is more real than what we cannot. Use numbers where they are helpful. Let qual­itative factors inform qualitative understanding. Don’t be imprisoned by the temptation to measure only what’s measurable.

1.1 The Principle

Modern management and policy are dominated by quantification. Numbers seem objective, comparable, and actionable. But systems have important properties — resilience, equity, quality, trust, morale, health — that resist easy quantification, or whose quantification misses what matters most.

Meadows’ warning is not anti-quantitative. She is not arguing that numbers are bad. She is arguing against the systematic neglect of important but hard-to-measure dimensions of system performance in favor of easy-to-measure proxies.

The problem has a name: proxy capture. When we substitute a measurable proxy for an important-but-unmeasured outcome, we end up optimizing the proxy at the expense of the outcome.

1.2 The Quantifiability Trap

Consider this progression:

  1. We care about learning (important, hard to measure)
  2. We measure test scores as a proxy for learning (measurable)
  3. We optimize for test scores
  4. Test scores improve; learning may or may not improve
  5. We declare success because the measure improved

This pattern recurs in:

  • Healthcare: optimizing for procedure rates vs. patient health
  • Criminal justice: optimizing for arrests and convictions vs. public safety
  • Environmental policy: optimizing for compliance paperwork vs. actual ecological health
  • Corporate governance: optimizing for quarterly earnings vs. long-term value

In each case, the system delivers what it is designed to deliver — high scores on the chosen metric — but potentially at the expense of what the metric was supposed to measure.

1.3 Important System Properties That Resist Quantification

Some crucial properties of systems are genuinely difficult to measure:

  • Resilience: the capacity of a system to absorb disturbance and reorganize while undergoing change. Resilience is often only visible when it is gone.

  • Trust: the foundation of social cooperation. Measured poorly (if at all) in real time, but its absence is catastrophic.

  • Legitimacy: whether institutions are accepted as valid by those subject to them. Difficult to measure; easy to destroy.

  • Ecosystem integrity: the health of a complex living system may not reveal itself in any single indicator.

  • Well-being: what people actually care about in life — meaning, relationships, health, autonomy — is systematically under-measured relative to income and consumption.

1.4 Examples

1.4.1 GDP and well-being

Gross Domestic Product measures the money value of goods and services produced. It was designed by its creators (including Simon Kuznets, who explicitly warned against this use) to measure economic production — not welfare. Yet GDP has become the primary proxy for national well-being in most policy discussions. Systems that grow GDP by depleting natural capital, increasing inequality, or degrading health may show strong “performance” on this metric while actually worsening the things people care about.

1.4.2 Hospital readmission rates

A widely used measure of hospital quality is 30-day readmission rates. Hospitals optimize to reduce readmissions. But hospitals in communities with strong social support networks and good primary care naturally have lower readmissions regardless of hospital quality. The metric punishes hospitals serving disadvantaged communities and rewards those in affluent ones, potentially inverting the intended signal.

1.4.3 Student evaluation of teaching

Students rate instructors on standardized scales. Research suggests these ratings correlate with entertainment value and grade expectations more strongly than with actual learning. Instructors who optimize for ratings may give easier grades and more entertaining but less rigorous lectures — improving the metric while reducing what the metric was supposed to capture.

ImportantGoodhart’s Law

“When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.”

— Attributed to Charles Goodhart (and related to Campbell’s Law and Goodhart’s Observation).

This principle captures a fundamental problem in complex systems: the act of measuring and targeting a metric changes the behavior of actors in the system in ways that undermine the measure’s validity.

1.5 Practical Application

  1. Ask: Why are we measuring this? What do we actually care about? What is the measure a proxy for? How well does it capture the thing we care about?

  2. Explicitly track the unmeasured. Identify 2–3 important but hard-to-measure dimensions of system performance. Find qualitative ways to track them — expert judgment, structured observation, narrative reports.

  3. Look for perverse optimization. Are actors in the system doing things that improve the metrics but degrade the underlying outcomes? This is a warning sign that the metrics have become decoupled from reality.

  4. Beware the “if you can’t measure it, you can’t manage it” fallacy. This widely repeated maxim is wrong. You can manage things you can’t fully measure — doctors did it before modern diagnostics; parents do it every day. The inability to measure perfectly is not a license to ignore.

TipKey Takeaway

The most important properties of a system are often the hardest to measure. Managing only what is measurable means systematically ignoring whatever resists quantification. Build explicit habits for tracking qualitative, hard-to-measure dimensions alongside the metrics.

1.6 Discussion Questions

  1. What are the most important unmeasured or poorly measured dimensions of performance in a system you care about? What would it cost to measure them better? What would be gained?

  2. Can you identify a case where optimizing a metric has degraded the underlying outcome the metric was supposed to capture?

  3. Meadows warns against “being imprisoned by the temptation to measure only what’s measurable.” In what organizational contexts is this temptation strongest? What drives it?

References

Meadows, Donella H. 2001. “Dancing with Systems.” Whole Earth. https://demorrison.ucdavis.edu/files/Meadows_Systems_Thinking_2001.pdf.
